# Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a heterogeneous group of chronic intestinal disorders in which microbial community structure, microbial function and host inflammation are coupled. Large cohort studies have established reproducible shifts in taxa, pathways and metabolites, while also showing that the magnitude and direction of those shifts depend on disease subtype, treatment, geography and sampling design [1–9]. These observations make microbiome-based classification attractive for cohort stratification and external validation, but they also make a high internal AUROC an incomplete description of a transferable disease signal.

The central problem is an information-boundary problem. A classifier can exploit abundance scale, taxonomic naming, repeated samples from the same participant, clinical measurements embedded in an omics table or the origin of a pooled cohort. Cross-study analyses and microbiome machine-learning benchmarks have therefore emphasized external validation, study-aware splitting and explicit confound auditing [10–12]. PRISM is a particularly useful example because its discovery and validation origins are biologically related but operationally distinct. A framework that pools these origins without an origin estimand can mistake cohort structure for disease signal, while a framework that includes age, faecal calprotectin or medication indicators in one branch but not another cannot support a like-for-like comparison.

A second boundary arises from the representation itself. Microbiome profiles are compositional: closure, sampling depth and zero handling affect the geometry available to a classifier [13–21]. Marginal models such as penalized logistic regression, random forests and boosting provide direct references for feature-level signal [27–30]. Relational models instead propagate information over a feature topology, and graph attention networks can learn unequal neighbour contributions [31–36]. Such a representation is scientifically plausible when microbial features are related, but the topology may also encode source-specific covariance. The relevant question is consequently not whether a graph network is generally better than a tabular model. It is whether, under a frozen feature contract and a declared source-to-target direction, the relational representation adds measurable information beyond strong marginal references.

Here we introduce a framework for making that question auditable. The framework has four design principles. First, the feature universe and metadata denylist are frozen before outcome modelling, and all scale transformations, prevalence filters and mutual-information selection are fitted within source-training participants. Second, participant-aware splits and participant-cluster bootstrap preserve the unit at which repeated observations arise. Third, synthetic known-truth controls, planted clinical leakage, PRISM origin-held-out transfer and topology nulls separate method validation from biological transfer. Fourth, a capacity-matched no-message comparator and paired model differences distinguish a relational contribution from generic nonlinear capacity or unpaired ranking noise. These principles connect compositional analysis [13–21], domain-shift evaluation [37], resampling and model-selection uncertainty [38–42] and prediction-reporting standards [43–45].

We apply the framework to HMP2/IBDMDB and PRISM as a worked IBD case study. The application is deliberately bounded: the exact-name metabolite intersection is reported, the primary cross-cohort analysis uses the shared microbiome space, the endpoint is IBD versus non-IBD, and graph-ranked taxa are treated as candidate features rather than validated biomarkers. The principal outputs are an auditable input and provenance contract, a directional relational-versus-tabular estimand, a set of validity and topology gates, and an empirical demonstration that relational gain can be forward-directional and cohort-dependent. This framing addresses the reviewers’ concerns about the GAT structural paradox, validation lookahead, PRISM pooling, baseline equivalence, effective sample size and overstated novelty while retaining the useful graph-versus-tabular scientific question.
